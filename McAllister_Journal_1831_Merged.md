# MEMORANDUM OF EVENTS
### of 1831
### by George C. McAllister

**Jan. 1831**

**Note 1st.**  The brig Keziah under my charge lying at Upton's Wharf loading. Very mild weather for the season, wind South Eastwardly.

**Note 2nd.**  Sunday. Attended Divine Service at the Methodist Chapel. Rev. Williams, Minister. Louisa married at 6 P.M. by Dr. Thompson. The brig in the care of Wm. Harper, Mate. W. northwestwardly.

**3rd.**  This day finished taking on the Deck load of Shingles, it being seven bunches in height, loading done, dropped down to the Ledge to prevent being froze in, anchored at the Ledge at 6 P.M. and returned home with Father in the carriage.

**Note 4th.**  Remained at home all this day it being cold, and much ice running in the River. Sent Horatio N. Hill to the Ledge to assist in looking after the Brig, while the Mate went to St. Andrews for his clothes.

**5th.**  Rode down to the Ledge to see about getting some small jobs done to the vessel necessary before going to sea. Wind Southwardly.

**6th.**  Rode down upon the American side and crossed over to Saint Andrews in the Brig's boat, to clear out for sea and ship two men, which done, crossed over to Robbinstown in the evening and returned home, leaving the boat to find her way to the Ledge. Wind Westwardly.

**Note 7th.**  This day at home till 9 P.M. when Horatio, Abner and myself bid farewell to our friends and walked to the Ledge, when after a long time and much trouble hailing, launching Boat, etc., we got on board the vessel at 1 A.M. on Saturday. Very cold. Wind North.

**Note 8th.**  All things ready at 5 A.M., weighed anchor and sailed down the River, passed Saint Andrews at Sunrising, and went out through Latete Passage, thence sailed down the American Coast. Crew, consisting of myself, Wm. Harper, mate, John Paul, John Earl, David Conolly, seamen, Jos. Irvin, cook, Horatio Hill, Abner McAllister and Reuben Whitney.

**9th.**  Sunday. No land in sight. Weather moderating, wind Light, snow. Corked in Bow port. 67° 10W.

**Jan. 1831**

**10th.**  Very thick snow storm. Ship Elizabeth from Saint Andrews passed us. Wind E. N.E. 66° 16.

**11th.**  Sleet and snow at intervals. Wind inclining to the Southward. 65° 11W. Lat. by Obs 40° 09N.

**12th.**  Weather moderate and wind light at E. by S. 64° 15W. Lat. 38° 25N.

**13th.**  This day a very strong gale from Northwest with a tremendous sea. Send the Brig S.E. as near as we could. She requires nearly one quarter of the compass to steer her in. Lat. 37° 05W. L. 62° 18.

**14th.**  Weather more moderate. Employed myself reading newspapers, writing, etc. 61° 18 Lat. 35° 03N.

**15th.**  This day the wind increased to a gale, lay the vessel to at ten A.M. Wind S.E. changed to S.W. 60° 12.

**16th.**  Sunday. A very severe gale, still continues laying to. The sea running tremendous high, causes the vessel to strain and make much water; pump constantly going. In the afternoon wind moderates a little and we attempted to send her — the wind increasing again to its former violence blew the foresail to pieces. Lay to again. Shipped a great quantity of water over the rail, and I expected for a time that she would sweep everything from the Deck. The sea broke the foreward deck load Stancheon, and two tiers of the shingles about half the way were lost. The day spent between hope and fear — a hope that we might survive the storm, and a fear that some of those dreadful seas might sweep us into oblivion. Long. 59° 00.

**17th.**  The gale still continues. The vessel laying to under a close reefed main topsail (throughout the gale). In the latter part of the day, it began to moderate. Everything completely drenched in water from forward to aft, not a corner but what the sea and rain has broke into — wind West, 58° 00.

**18th.**  The wind is light and variable attended with squalls of rain and sunshine at intervals. We made sail again after a gale of seventy hours, the longest that ever I experienced at sea, and I don't know but what the strongest, as I never saw a vessel roll her lower yards so near the water as at this time, when they were not more than five or six feet above it, and the water about three feet on the lee rail. No weather to dry any clothes therefore everything continues wet, beds not excepted. This scene would not compare well with the privileges and enjoyments at home especially that of Sunday. As a boy says "if he were as sick at home as here he should be in bed", while here he cannot even remain in bed when permitted. Wind west southwardly — Long. 57° 30. Lat. 33° 37W.

**19th.**  Weather very unsettled. At noon in a very heavy squall of wind and rain, the wheel unshipt, the vessel brought too parted the Foot Rope of the Fore Top sail, and blew it to pieces. **23rd.**  Sunday. Very pleasant and the air clear and salubrious. Wind Southwest. Lat. 26° 39 Long. 55° 08W.

**25th.**  Calm first part of the day with a long rolling sea. Wrought upon a sail part of this day and attended to the duties of the vessel devolving on me. Reading, etc. West Lat. 24° 38. Long. 54° 30W.

**26th.**  Weather fine and wind variable. A Boil on my wrist a troublesome companion. Overhauled our stock of bread and moved it, to get it out of the way of rats. 22° 29 Long. 54° 00.

**Mar. 1831**

**13th.**  Sunday. Sent the mate and all hands on shore to load the Flat if the molasses were ready. At 10 A.M. the boat returned finding no molasses ready, sent 12 casks on shore. The planter is boiling sugar this morning to make out the proper quantity for the week. The process by which sugar is made appears simple and easy, after the liquid is pressed from the cane, it passes by means of a spout into the first boiler, out of which it is dipped into a second, then to a third, and to a fourth (when the process is final) out of which it is again dipped and put into a reservoir to cool, and thence dipped again into a large shallow cistern, from whence, after it gets hard and cold, it is shovelled up and put into casks to drain the molasses from the sugar, and so cure it. The whole time of boiling is one hour for each process. The liquid has a very dirty appearance, worse than soap and water would have until the limewater is put into it, which is in the third and fourth Boilers, which immediately changed its colour from that of dirty water to a clear yellow. During the process the liquid is passed three times through a fine strainer. An Estate here of 40 slaves great and small is allowed to yield annually 200 hogsheads of sugar containing 1500 lbs. each. The planters are mostly French descendants and consequently speak very little English. The wind has shifted to-day to S.E. the first time for eight days, having been N.E. mostly. The men and boys ashore in the afternoon at an English Estate. The Planters life at Point Cedar is really miserable, being no comparison in my opinion to the seaman's or lumberman's. He is excluded from all society, either pious or social, excepting the slaves of the plantation who he drives about like cattle, Sunday and Monday, he is employed himself constantly in looking after the affairs of the Estate, there being but one white person on each of the Estates that I have been at. There is no road excepting the Beach at low water, or a path through a corn patch, consequently very little communication. And when they are not able to attend to the Duties incumbent, they are no longer wanted. Weather very fine this day.

**14th.**  Sent again for the Molasses and got 13 casks, went in the afternoon and got 12 more. A great surf in the afternoon being full sea. 25 casks.

Such as I saw was about as hard as dry clay, of an oily appearance, but have been told it is very soft and sulphurous at the Pitch Lake. We smelled it as we passed. Came to an anchor in the evening to wait the tide. I think it is a plain proof of what I have often asserted of the West Indies, that if a man is not able to help himself there is nobody knows him, and the contrary everybody is willing to serve him. Dear Sir:— I was disappointed in finding the Brig uninsured on my arrival at Saint Andrews, as it was a much greater risk than I expected, the ice being almost an insuperable obstacle in the way. The following extracts are from letters written by Hon. George S. Hill to his wife —

Fredericton, 8th. March, 1842.

"I had learned the melancholy fate of poor McAllister before your last letter, but did not mention it, as too unpleasant a subject to write to. The circumstances are announced in the St. John paper. At first was a gleam of hope — though very slight — that there might be some mistake in the account. If he had arrived in safety, Mr. Lindsay must have had a letter from him, before the date of yours. His having received none is a sad confirmation of the truth of the account. Which must cast a sad gloom not only on his immediate friends, but the whole neighborhood. Poor Louisa! It is a sad stroke to her".

12th March. "Poor McAllister! I cannot help often thinking of him and his untimely fate, and the bereavement of his family and friends".

Extract of letter from David Upton to Hon. George S. Hill in Fredericton dated Saint Stephen, Feby. 27, 1842.

"But we have bad news to-day — poor McAllister was lost overboard from the "Eliza Ann" in the dreadful gale of the 16 — on the coast of Nova Scotia. Being fearful of some accident Mr. Lindsay sent to St. Andrews last eve to get the St. John mail and receive a letter from Capt. Killam of Yarmouth, dated 22nd., stating that "a report reached here yesterday that the Brig Eliza Ann with produce from the West Indies for Halifax, got in Argyle dismasted on Saturday last. Captain, one seaman and a passenger was lost overboard in the gale of the 16th." Capt. Killam is a credible man, and there can hardly be a doubt but the report is true.

He sailed from Mat. . . . on the 2 Feby. in company with a Brig from Boston, which vessel reached Boston on Monday the 14th, consequently he would be close upon the coast at the time of the gale, with a hold full of glasses & sugar & some ten or twenty casks on deck.

Poor Mac. We shall miss him, his was a hard fortune, though all his days upon the ocean — yet he never sailed, but to encounter gales, disasters, or tornadoes — and that element with which he grappled in almost every form of terror and of shipwreck has at length proved the conqueror".

Monday 28.

We have further accounts from St. John this day confirming the report, but they all come by way of Yarmouth as above. On Wednesday we shall most likely have particulars. Poor Louisa is in a dreadful state of nervous and mental excitement.

A commission was appointed by the Governor to clear the streets etc., with full power and authority to call on all house-holders, who are owners of slaves, to furnish labour for that purpose, and to enforce the attendance thereof by every means, and to associate with themselves all architects resident in Bridgetown. Nov. 11th.  Mrs. Peter Christie, sister of Mrs. Aaron Upton, lived on the East side of King Street. McAllister, apparently, stayed with Mrs. Christie from this time on, when he spent the night at Saltwater.

15th.  Joel Hill, a cousin of McAllister, born 1796, married Mary Todd. The house is above Milltown, opposite the old reservoir. This was a favorite place for dances and parties for many years.
In a letter to his brother George S. Hill, at Hanover, New Hampshire, dated October 19, 1816, Joel, almost twenty years old, writes: "I am now about to begin for myself. Father has given me my time, and is to supply me with provisions and oxen for logging this season. I am going to carry on with Patch and Sanborn. We calculate to start next Monday, and if nothing happens more than common, we shall not return with our teams till the middle of May. It has been about twelve weeks since the Brig sailed for the West Indies, and we have heard nothing from her at all, but we expect her in now every day."
The old house still stands on the shoulder of Todd's Mountain, looking across the St. Croix to Maguerrewok Mountain and Meadows down river to Devil's Head and the Chamcooks in the distance. The house has been modernized — a spindly one flue chimney in the middle of the roof, where the old chimney probably had six flues — boarding-house windows replace the small panes — and, of course, some sort of tar paper roof as our local law-givers do not allow us to use cedar shingles! The daughter, Mary, married George A. Boardman, "The Naturalist of the St. Croix". Joel's son, Charles E., has some descendants in Fredericton.
